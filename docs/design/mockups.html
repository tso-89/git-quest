Three ways to teach git

One learner: someone who has never run git init, who is now working alongside an AI agent that writes hundreds of lines an hour. They need version control more urgently than any generation of beginner before them, and they have less patience for a manual. Same curriculum in all three directions below — what changes is the room you learn it in.

Chapter Five

Three trays on a desk

Where a change sits before it becomes history

Picture a clerk with three trays. The first holds everything currently on the desk — scribbled on, crossed out, half-finished. That is your working directory, and git watches it but promises nothing about it.

The second tray is the shortlist: the pages the clerk has decided belong in today's entry. Moving a page there is git add. Nothing is permanent yet — you can take a page back out.

The third tray is the bound ledger, and it only ever grows. git commit copies the shortlist into it, stamps it with the date, your name, and a sentence explaining why. That sentence is the whole point.

Notice what did not move. secrets.env stayed on the desk, and that is correct — some pages never go in the ledger at all. Chapter three explains how to make git refuse to file them even if you ask.

In the margin
Why the sentence matters In six months the only thing standing between you and total confusion is the message you wrote today. "fix stuff" helps nobody, least of all an agent reading your history for context.
The ledger only grows Almost nothing in git is ever really deleted. That is why chapter six can promise you that any mistake is recoverable.

05

What I would build, and why

These are not mutually exclusive in the way they look. The real question is which surface leads — and the honest answer is that the curriculum changes shape halfway through. Chapters 00–07 are a beginner learning fundamentals. Chapters 08–09 are that same person reviewing a machine's work.

Recommendation

A, growing into C. Open as Terminal Quest — one surface, one face, low intimidation, real commands from minute one. Then at chapter 08 the layout earns its third pane: the agent panel slides in and the room becomes the Workbench. The lesson levels up at exactly the point the learner does.

Pick B instead if…

the audience is genuinely non-technical — a writer, a designer, a founder — and the goal is "stop being afraid of this" rather than "be productive by Friday".

Pick C alone if…

the learner already writes code and is only here because an agent is now committing on their behalf. Skip the gentle on-ramp; go straight to diff review.

@@ what v1.0 ships either way @@
+A single static page — no build step, no server, no account. Opens from a file.
+A working sandbox git engine: real repo state, staging, commits, branches, merges, reflog.
+A live commit graph that redraws as the learner types commands.
+Progress saved locally, so chapter 06 is still waiting tomorrow.
+Agent picker driving chapters 08–09, with a rules-file generator you can copy out.
+Keyboard operable, screen-reader labelled, and readable in light and dark.
